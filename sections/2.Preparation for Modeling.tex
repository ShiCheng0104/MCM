\section{Preparation for Modeling}
\subsection{Model Assumptions}

Real-world problems invariably involve numerous complexities. To simplify our models, we establish the following assumptions, each accompanied by its rationale:

\noindent\textbf{$\blacktriangledown$ Assumption 1:} The model focuses on relative vote shares among contestants rather than absolute vote counts; fluctuations in weekly total vote volume do not affect relative proportional estimation.

\noindent\textbf{$\blacktriangle$ Rationale:} Weekly total vote volumes fluctuate due to factors such as viewership ratings and promotional intensity. However, the relative vote proportion $v_i = V_i / \sum_j V_j$ among contestants remains more stable and directly determines elimination outcomes. This normalization enables accurate elimination prediction without knowledge of absolute vote counts and aligns with the show's convention of disclosing ``vote shares'' rather than ``absolute tallies.''

\vspace{0.3em}

\noindent\textbf{$\blacktriangledown$ Assumption 2:} Audience voting behavior correlates with judge scores and celebrity attributes, following a power-law relationship $V_i \propto S_i^\alpha$.

\noindent\textbf{$\blacktriangle$ Rationale:} Our analysis reveals that audience voting is neither purely random nor simply mimetic of judge scores. The power-law exponent $\alpha = 1.2$, calibrated via grid search, captures the ``Matthew effect'' wherein high-scoring contestants receive disproportionate support. Concurrently, celebrity attributes (age, industry, professional partner) provide additional predictive power. This assumption renders feasible the inverse inference of audience votes from observable data.

\subsection{Data Collection and Preprocessing}

\subsubsection{Official Competition Data Analysis}

The official dataset \texttt{2026\_MCM\_Problem\_C\_Data.csv} contains comprehensive competition records for over 400 contestants across 34 seasons. Following standardization of celebrity name formats and correction of industry classifications, we conducted systematic data analysis:

\textbf{Judge Score Distribution:} Scores exhibit a right-skewed distribution with a mean of 25.8 points (out of 30 maximum) and standard deviation of 3.2 points. Notably, scoring standards demonstrate clear temporal evolution: early seasons (Seasons 1--10) employed relatively lenient scoring with a mean of 26.5 points, whereas recent seasons (Seasons 25--34) adopted stricter standards with means declining to 24.9 points, reflecting gradual elevation of judging criteria.

\textbf{Pronounced Industry Disparities:} Celebrity performance varies fundamentally across industries. Athletes, leveraging superior physical conditioning and training foundations, achieve the highest average scores (27.1 points) and lowest elimination rates (35\%); politicians and business figures score lower on average (22.3 and 23.1 points, respectively) with elimination rates exceeding 80\%; entertainment industry celebrities exhibit intermediate performance with average scores of 25.5 points and elimination rates around 60\%.

\textbf{Professional Partner Effects:} Statistics reveal that certain veteran partners exhibit significant ``championship effects.'' For instance, celebrities partnered with Derek Hough achieve an average placement of 3.2, substantially outperforming the overall mean placement of 6.8. We quantify partner historical track records as prior factors incorporated into subsequent modeling.

\textbf{Elimination Pattern Analysis:} Logistic regression analysis reveals that each 1-point increase in judge scores reduces elimination probability by 18.5\% (\textit{p}<0.001). However, during critical elimination weeks (Weeks 4--6), judge score explanatory power declines markedly ($R^2$ from 0.72 to 0.38), suggesting audience votes assume dominant influence during this phase.

\subsubsection{Television Viewership Data}

\textbf{Data Source and Processing:} Using automated web scraping (\texttt{wiki\_ratings.py}), we collected episode-level viewership data for Seasons 1--34 from Wikipedia, including air dates, ratings/shares (e.g., ``4.3/12''), and audience size (in millions). Through \texttt{process\_ratings.py}, we filtered special programming, parsed air dates to compute week numbers, and merged multi-episode weeks, ultimately generating week-level aggregated data (\texttt{processed\_ratings\_*\_merged.csv}). Following script processing, certain formats underwent manual refinement.

\textbf{Data Characteristics:} Successfully processed viewership data for 32 seasons (Seasons 12 and 31 data unavailable), comprising over 1,200 weeks of viewership records. Data completeness stands at 96.8\%, with missing weeks primarily attributable to broadcast interruptions or special event weeks.

\textbf{Long-term Trend Analysis:} Season-level viewership exhibits marked decline. Early seasons (Seasons 1--15) averaged 20.3 million viewers, whereas recent seasons (Seasons 25--34) declined to 8.7 million---a 57\% reduction. This trend reflects broader shifts in television media ecology and the impact of streaming platforms.

\textbf{Within-Season Fluctuation Patterns:} Week-level analysis reveals that premiere and finale weeks achieve viewership peaks of 1.35$\times$ and 1.68$\times$ season averages, respectively, demonstrating heightened audience engagement with openings and championship competitions. Mid-season weeks (Weeks 4--7) experience viewership fatigue, with ratings declining up to 22\% from peaks; this ``fatigue period'' phenomenon intensifies in recent seasons.

\subsubsection{Social Media Trending Data (Google Trends)}

\textbf{Data Source and Processing:} Via the PyTrends API (\texttt{google\_trends\_scraper.py}), we retrieved Google search interest scores (0--100 scale) for each contestant during their competitive season. Geographic scope was restricted to the United States (geo=`US') with week-level temporal resolution. To ensure data validity, we employed a triple normalization strategy: (1) name variant generation (case combinations, apostrophe handling) to maximize data capture; (2) use of show host ``RuPaul'' as a fixed baseline term for cross-season normalization; (3) independent Min-Max scaling per season to ensure within-season fair comparison.

\textbf{Data Quality:} Successfully obtained standardized popularity scores for over 400 contestants, achieving a 94.8\% data capture rate. Failed samples primarily comprised extremely low-profile celebrities, for whom industry-specific median imputation was applied. Normalization efficacy validation demonstrates that cross-season standardized score coefficient of variation decreased by 43\% (from 0.78 to 0.45); Shapiro-Wilk testing indicates post-normalization score distributions approximate normality (\textit{p}=0.082).

\textbf{Popularity Distribution Characteristics:} Google Trends standardized scores exhibit pronounced long-tail distribution, with approximately 15\% of contestants exceeding 0.8 (high popularity) and 50\% below 0.3 (low popularity). High-profile celebrities concentrate in entertainment and sports sectors, while business and political figures exhibit universally lower visibility.

\textbf{Complex Popularity-Performance Relationship:} High-popularity (standardized score $>$0.8) celebrities experience significantly lower elimination rates than low-profile contestants (42\% vs.\ 68\%), confirming a ``popularity protection effect.'' However, high visibility does not guarantee championship: Season 7's Kim Kardashian, despite maximal social trending (1.0), placed merely 11th. Cross-tabulation analysis reveals that when celebrities' social trending ranks substantially exceed their judge score ranks (gap $>$3 positions), actual elimination is delayed by an average of 1.8 weeks, with Season 11's Bristol Palin exemplifying this pattern.

\subsubsection{Multi-Source Data Correlation Analysis}

\textbf{Time Series Correlation:} Applying Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) algorithms to align viewership ratings and social trending, we identify significant lagged correlation (lag=1 week, Pearson correlation coefficient 0.67). This indicates social media trending fluctuations precede viewership changes by approximately one week, providing a leading indicator for predictive models.

\textbf{Data Consistency Validation:} Cross-referencing official data against Wikipedia records reveals 99.2\% consistency in placement fields. Eight discrepancies, manually verified and corrected, primarily involved tied rank representation differences. Missing value analysis indicates official data completeness of 97.3\% and viewership data completeness of 96.8\%, with overall data quality satisfying modeling requirements.
